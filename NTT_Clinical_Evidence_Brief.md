# The Science Behind NTT & Timeline Transformation Therapy™
### A clinical evidence brief for mental-health professionals considering a referral

**Prepared for practitioners who want to understand the neuroscience and peer-reviewed research that underpins Neuro Transformation Therapy (NTT) and its core change process, Timeline Transformation Therapy™ (TTT).**

---

## How to read this document

NTT is an **integrative coaching and change modality**. Like EMDR, clinical hypnosis, imagery rescripting, and schema work, it doesn't invent new biology — it *applies* well-established mechanisms of how the brain forms, stores, retrieves and updates emotional memory. This brief is organised by **mechanism**: for each thing NTT actually does with a client, we show (a) what happens in the session and (b) the published science that explains *why it works*.

A note on honesty, because it is what actually builds credibility with clinicians: NTT as a *branded program* has not itself been run through randomised controlled trials, and we don't claim it has. What is heavily evidenced is the **set of mechanisms it uses**. That is the honest and defensible way to present this to a referring clinician — and it is a stronger position than overclaiming, because every reference below is checkable.

## 1. Working with the "subconscious mind": memory, emotion and the non-conscious brain

**What NTT says:** Beliefs, emotional responses and behavioural patterns are stored below conscious awareness ("the subconscious"), and lasting change requires working at that level rather than through conscious willpower alone.

**The science:** Modern neuroscience doesn't use the word "subconscious," but it strongly supports the underlying idea — that most emotional learning and behaviour is driven by **non-conscious, implicit memory systems** (the amygdala, basal ganglia and related limbic circuits) that operate outside deliberate control.

- **LeDoux, J. (1996/2015)** — *The Emotional Brain* and later work established that the amygdala forms emotional (fear) associations rapidly and implicitly, and that these drive reactions before the conscious cortex is involved. This is precisely the mechanism your manual describes as the amygdala "locking in" an emotion and meaning to an event.
- **Shin, L.M. & Liberzon, I. (2010).** "The neurocircuitry of fear, stress, and anxiety disorders." *Neuropsychopharmacology, 35,* 169–191. — Maps how implicit fear circuitry (amygdala, hippocampus, prefrontal cortex) underlies anxiety and trauma reactions.
- **van der Kolk, B. (2014).** *The Body Keeps the Score.* — The mainstream clinical reference for how trauma is held in non-verbal, body-and-limbic memory rather than conscious narrative, and why "talking about it" alone is often insufficient. This is the single best book to hand a mental-health referrer.

**Take-away for a clinician:** "Working with the subconscious" translates cleanly into "working with implicit emotional memory and limbic conditioning" — a completely mainstream target of trauma-focused therapy.

## 2. The trauma model: how an event gets "locked in" — and how it can be unlocked

**What NTT says:** A trauma is an emotional overload the nervous system couldn't process at the time; the amygdala binds an emotion and a limiting meaning to the memory, which then gets re-triggered in the present.

**The science — this is the strongest part of the case.** The mechanism that lets an old, "locked-in" emotional memory be changed is called **memory reconsolidation**, and it is one of the most robust findings in affective neuroscience of the last 25 years.

- **Nader, K., Schafe, G.E., & LeDoux, J.E. (2000).** "Fear memories require protein synthesis in the amygdala for reconsolidation after retrieval." *Nature, 406,* 722–726. — The landmark study showing that when a consolidated fear memory is *reactivated*, it briefly becomes labile and can be re-written before it re-stabilises. This is the biological basis for why revisiting a memory (as TTT does) opens a window to change it.
- **Schiller, D., Monfils, M-H., Raio, C.M., Johnson, D.C., LeDoux, J.E., & Phelps, E.A. (2010).** "Preventing the return of fear in humans using reconsolidation update mechanisms." *Nature, 463,* 49–53. — Demonstrated the same reconsolidation-updating effect **in humans**, non-pharmacologically, by introducing new information during the reactivation window.
- **Ecker, B., Ticic, R., & Hulley, L. (2012).** *Unlocking the Emotional Brain: Eliminating Symptoms at Their Roots Using Memory Reconsolidation.* Routledge. — Translates the neuroscience into a clinical "transformation sequence": reactivate the emotional learning → introduce a contradictory/mismatching experience → the memory's emotional charge dissolves. **This maps almost step-for-step onto what TTT does** (return to the root-cause memory, extract the "lesson/learning" that contradicts the old meaning, and release the emotion). Hand this to any clinician who wants the mechanism.
- **Lane, R.D., Ryan, L., Nadel, L., & Greenberg, L. (2015).** "Memory reconsolidation, emotional arousal, and the process of change in psychotherapy." *Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 38,* e1. — A peer-reviewed argument that reconsolidation is a **common mechanism of change across all effective psychotherapies**, requiring emotional arousal plus new learning at the moment of recall. This legitimises the "feel it, then re-learn it" structure of NTT.

**Take-away:** The claim "we go back to the root memory and the emotion dissolves" is not mystical — it is textbook memory-reconsolidation therapy. This is the citation set that will most impress a clinically-trained referrer.

## 3. Timeline / regression work and the "lessons" step: extinction and inhibitory learning

**What NTT says:** The practitioner has the client fly above their timeline, revisit the first event, take the *positive learning/lesson*, and the negative emotion no longer fires.

**The science:** Extracting a corrective learning that makes the old emotional response unnecessary is the core of **inhibitory learning**, the current scientific account of how exposure and extinction actually work.

- **Craske, M.G., Treanor, M., Conway, C.C., Zbozinek, T., & Vervliet, B. (2014).** "Maximizing exposure therapy: An inhibitory learning approach." *Behaviour Research and Therapy, 58,* 10–23. — The old fear memory isn't erased; a **new, competing "safety/learning" memory** is formed that inhibits it. This is exactly the function of NTT's "get the lesson" step.
- **EMDR as a close clinical cousin.** Eye Movement Desensitisation and Reprocessing also has clients hold a distressing memory while doing bilateral/attentional work, then install an adaptive belief — structurally very similar to timeline regression. EMDR is recommended for PTSD by the **World Health Organization (2013)** and NICE, and supported by meta-analyses (e.g. **Bisson et al., 2013, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews**). Citing EMDR shows a referrer that "revisit-the-memory-and-reprocess-it" is an established, guideline-endorsed family of methods.

## 4. Visualisation / mental imagery: why "imagining it" changes the brain

**What NTT says:** Visualisation and imagined rehearsal give access to the emotional brain and can install new emotional realities.

**The science:** This is one of the best-supported claims in the whole model. The brain treats **vividly imagined events much like real ones** — the same sensory, emotional and motor circuits activate.

- **Pearson, J. (2019).** "The human imagination: the cognitive neuroscience of visual mental imagery." *Nature Reviews Neuroscience, 20,* 624–634. — Imagery recruits overlapping neural machinery with actual perception and can drive learning and emotion.
- **Kosslyn, S.M., Ganis, G., & Thompson, W.L. (2001).** "Neural foundations of imagery." *Nature Reviews Neuroscience, 2,* 635–642.
- **Reddan, M.C., Wager, T.D., & Schiller, D. (2018).** "Attenuating Neural Threat Expression with Imagination." *Neuron, 100(4),* 994–1005. — Strikingly, **imagined** exposure to a threat cue reduced threat responses in the brain *as effectively as real exposure*. Direct neural evidence that visualisation can extinguish fear — a perfect citation for the visualisation-based parts of TTT.
- **Ji, J.L., Holmes, E.A., et al. (2016).** "Emotional Mental Imagery as Simulation of Reality." *Behavior Therapy, 47(5),* 702–719; and **Holmes, E.A. & Mathews, A. (2010).** "Mental imagery in emotion and emotional disorders." *Clinical Psychology Review, 30(3),* 349–362. — Imagery has a stronger, more direct link to emotion than verbal thought, which is *why* imagery-based methods move feelings that talk-therapy struggles to shift.
- **Morina, N., Lancee, J., & Arntz, A. (2017).** "Imagery rescripting as a clinical intervention for aversive memories: A meta-analysis." *Journal of Behavior Therapy and Experimental Psychiatry, 55,* 6–15; and **Arntz, A. (2012),** *Journal of Experimental Psychopathology, 3(2),* 189–208. — **Imagery Rescripting** — going back into a memory in imagination and changing its meaning/outcome — is an evidence-based clinical technique with meta-analytic support. This is the mainstream clinical name for what TTT does, and is a very useful term for Lori to use with clinicians.
- **Ranganathan, V.K., et al. (2004).** "From mental power to muscle power—gaining strength by using the mind." *Neuropsychologia, 42(7),* 944–956. — A concrete, memorable demonstration that imagined practice alone produces measurable physical change (strength gains), proving the brain acts on imagined rehearsal.

## 5. The relaxed, inward-focused state (Hakalau / "trance"): brainwaves and heightened plasticity

**What NTT says:** The work is done in a relaxed, eyes-closed, inwardly-focused state that lowers brainwave activity toward slower (theta) rhythms and opens access to the subconscious.

**The science — with one accuracy note.** Deeply relaxed, absorbed, hypnosis-like states *are* associated with a measurable shift in EEG toward **slower alpha and theta rhythms**, and theta is specifically tied to memory encoding and neural plasticity. One caution for credibility: frame this as **alpha/theta**, not "delta." Delta dominates deep *unconscious* sleep, so claiming conscious therapeutic access "at delta" is the one place a neurophysiologist would push back. Stay with theta/alpha and you are on solid, citable ground:

- **Jamieson, G.A. & Burgess, A.P. (2014).** "Hypnotic induction is followed by state-like changes in the organization of EEG functional connectivity in the theta and beta frequency bands." *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, 8,* 528.
- **Sabourin, M.E., Cutcomb, S.D., Crawford, H.J., & Pribram, K. (1990).** "EEG correlates of hypnotic susceptibility and hypnotic trance: spectral analysis and coherence." *International Journal of Psychophysiology, 10(2),* 125–142. — Highly responsive subjects show **more theta** during trance.
- **Landry, M., Lifshitz, M., & Raz, A. (2017).** "Brain correlates of hypnosis: A systematic review and meta-analytic exploration." *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews, 81,* 75–98. — A rigorous overview of the neuroscience of hypnotic states.
- **Buzsáki, G. (2002).** "Theta oscillations in the hippocampus." *Neuron, 33,* 325–340. — Establishes theta rhythm's central role in memory formation and synaptic plasticity — i.e. *why* a theta-dominant state is a good state in which to update emotional memories.

**On clinical hypnosis efficacy** (since NTT's language patterns and inductions draw on it):
- **Thompson, T., et al. (2019).** "The effectiveness of hypnosis for pain relief: A systematic review and meta-analysis of 85 controlled experimental trials." *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews, 99,* 298–310.
- **Häuser, W., Hagl, M., Schmierer, A., & Hansen, E. (2016).** "The efficacy, safety and applications of medical hypnosis." *Deutsches Ärzteblatt International, 113(17),* 289–296.

## 6. Neuroplasticity: why new beliefs and behaviours can be "installed" and made to stick

**What NTT says:** After clearing the old pattern, new beliefs, behaviours and future goals are installed and rehearsed.

**The science:** The brain physically rewires with focused, repeated, emotionally-charged experience — "neurons that fire together, wire together" (**Hebb, 1949**).

- **Doidge, N. (2007).** *The Brain That Changes Itself.* — The accessible mainstream reference for experience-dependent neuroplasticity; good to recommend to clients and referrers alike.
- The reconsolidation and inhibitory-learning references in Sections 2–3 are the mechanism by which the *new* learning stabilises. Repetition and emotional intensity (your manual's "the oven") are exactly the conditions plasticity research says strengthen new encoding.

## 7. Intergenerational / "inherited" patterns: the epigenetics your manual already references

**What NTT says:** Some fears and patterns can be passed down across generations (your manual notes "they did studies with mice").

**The science — this is real, and the mouse study exists:**
- **Dias, B.G. & Ressler, K.J. (2014).** "Parental olfactory experience influences behavior and neural structure in subsequent generations." *Nature Neuroscience, 17(1),* 89–96. — The exact mouse study: a conditioned fear was transmitted to offspring epigenetically. Cite this directly.
- **Yehuda, R., et al. (2016).** "Holocaust exposure induced intergenerational effects on FKBP5 methylation." *Biological Psychiatry, 80(5),* 372–380. — Human evidence of epigenetic transmission of trauma-related stress biology.

## 8. Rapport, expectancy and the therapeutic relationship

**What NTT says:** Rapport, belief in the process, and the client's decision to change are essential.

**The science:** Across *all* modalities, the **therapeutic alliance and response expectancy** are among the largest predictors of outcome.
- **Wampold, B.E. (2015).** "How important are the common factors in psychotherapy? An update." *World Psychiatry, 14(3),* 270–277.
- **Benedetti, F. (2014).** *Placebo Effects.* Oxford University Press. — The neuroscience of how expectation alone produces real, measurable brain and body changes.

This is why NTT's heavy emphasis on rapport, pre-framing and the client's committed "decision to change" is not fluff — it is mobilising the best-evidenced non-specific factors in all of psychotherapy.

*All references above are real, published, and independently verifiable. Where an NTT claim outruns the evidence (e.g. "delta"), it's flagged rather than papered over — because with a clinical audience, accuracy is the credibility.*
